## Understanding Batch Auctions

Most electronic markets, crypto exchanges included, run on a continuous limit
order book (CLOB). Orders rest in a book sorted by price, and when a new order
crosses the spread it trades immediately against the best resting quote. Ties
at the same price go to whoever arrived first. In a continuous limit order book, every fill, quote, and cancel is processed one at a time, in arrival order.

Processing by arrival order creates a clear incentive: if the market moves, be the first
to react. When a news event or external factor changes
the fair price, a market maker's resting quote can become stale. If it does, a race begins. Whoever hits that stale
quote first can extract profit from its mispricing. Inside the race, the maker's cancel is a single message trying to outpace every sniper's order. More often than not, the maker's stale quote gets picked off. These latency races, often referred to as sniping or latency arbitrage, are a form of
adverse selection that concentrates on market makers. In response, makers size down and widen their tightest quotes, and
this thinner, widened spread is an added cost paid by everyone trading for ordinary reasons.

[Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/137/1/493/6368348)
measured the race directly, using exchange message data that records the
losers as well as the winners. In FTSE 100 stocks they found races running
about once per minute per symbol, typically decided in five to ten microseconds, accounting for roughly 20% of trading volume, with the top six
firms on one side or the other of more than 80% of them. Eliminating latency
arbitrage, they estimate, would cut the market's cost of liquidity by 17%,
about $5 billion a year in global equities. This additional liquidity cost is structurally inherent to
the design of continuous order books.

Frequent batch auctions are the design change
[Budish, Cramton, and Shim proposed](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/130/4/1547/1916146):
instead of processing orders continuously, collect them for a short window,
seal it, and clear every crossing order at one uniform price. Inside a window
there is no first. A momentarily stale quote is no longer a prize for the
fastest, because while the auction window is open, everyone has the same
chance to react. A microsecond-level latency advantage no longer matters. With that form of adverse selection neutralized, a maker can quote
tighter and with larger size.

The design space has kept moving since. Jump Crypto's 2025
[dual-flow batch auction](https://jumpcrypto.com/resources/dual-flow-batch-auction)
splits each window into two auctions, maker bids against taker sells and maker
asks against taker buys, so taker flow always trades against standing quotes
instead of netting against itself. While the _Snapshot Fees_ mechanism proposed in this piece could work for any batch auction implementation, it is likely a more natural fit for a dual-flow batch auction. The reasoning behind that will be covered later.

Batch auctions improve upon continuous books by neutralizing latency arbitrage and the widened spreads it forces. Structurally, operating within discrete time windows provides an additional feature continuous books cannot offer: a clean snapshot of the market's state, with each order in it committed and fillable at that instant. This piece asks whether that snapshot could be used to price orders directly, and whether pricing them well could improve the market further, with even tighter quotes and more size behind them.

## Trading Fees Today

Exchanges everywhere charge for execution, and nearly everywhere the same two
ideas shape the price: liquidity provision is treated better than liquidity
demand, and activity is rewarded, with rates that fall as volume grows. In
traditional markets these arrangements are more layered and bespoke, running
through negotiated maker programs and rebate schedules that vary by venue and
firm. This piece focuses on crypto markets, where fee structures are simpler
and public, already quoted in bps, and the most congruent with what it
proposes.

There, the typical schedule is compact: a maker/taker split, where takers pay
a few bps of notional and makers pay less, sometimes nothing, occasionally a
rebate. Tiers then scale those rates with each account's trailing volume. As a design this
is a reasonable proxy: volume is easy to measure and roughly tracks how much
value a participant brings to a venue. On a continuous book it is also close
to the only thing an exchange can price. There is no moment when the market
stands still to be judged, so fees attach to the one thing that accumulates:
what you traded.

Batch auctions do have such a moment: every window ends with the market
sealed and judgeable. Conventional fee standards like volume-based pricing
still work here, but the sealed window makes a different kind of pricing
possible. Jump Crypto's dual-flow paper alludes to this: maker fees could be
priced by how far an order stands from the midpoint. This piece takes that
idea and expands it into a full pricing mechanism.

## Pricing Each Auction's Liquidity

If the objective is to use fees to improve the market's quotes, then we can price that
directly:

> Liquidity standing on one side of the book pays the cap, the same rate a
> market order pays. Liquidity standing on both sides pays less. Liquidity
> standing on both sides, close to the mid, pays the least.

That is the entire mechanism of _Snapshot Fees_: re-computed per auction and deterministically derived from its sealed state.

This has the potential to unlock several key benefits:

- **Day-one competition.** A new maker can pay the best rates from their
  first window. Since the rate follows the
  quality of the quotes, a newcomer willing to quote tight and two-sided
  can compete with the largest incumbents immediately.
- **Per-market competition.** Fees are calculated market by market, window by
  window. A maker's standing in one market buys nothing in another, so every
  market, however small, is independently contestable: a specialist quoting
  one obscure pair well competes on equal terms with the largest firm in it.

Together, these two make liquidity supply responsive. When interest arrives
in an asset, makers can arrive with it at full strength without the need to
build up history first.

The rest of this piece builds out _Snapshot Fees_ one layer at a time, with a playable
lab at each step: the price of placement for a single order, then a full
one-maker book, then the market-wide version.

## The Base Fee

For liquidity nearest the mid to pay the least, distance from the mid has to
carry a price. The base fee is the first leg of that price: it sets the
lowest rate a resting order can be charged given its distance from the market's midpoint, referred to as the mark price,
$M$, which is calculated from the sealed book. Whether an order
actually gets that rate is the rest of the mechanism's job.

The schedule around the mark takes a simple shape. Nearest to $M$ sits a band
with a fee level of its own, zero in everything this piece shows. Beyond the
band's edge the fee grows with distance, at rates the market configures; under the
default calibration used here it grows gently through the zone where makers do much of their
everyday quoting, then more steeply past it. The fee never exceeds the
cap, which is the cap the rule referred to: $F$, the same rate a market order
pays. No resting order is ever charged more than a taker.

Making that shape precise takes seven values, of three kinds.

The first value is dynamic and recomputed every window:

- $M$, the Mark Price: the market's midpoint, calculated from the sealed book.
  Its formula is introduced in [Calculating the Mark](#calculating-the-mark)
  below, and the sections after it refine how the measurement works.

The market's configuration settings, fixed from window to window, though the
regions they define all sit relative to $M$:

- $B$, the Inner Band: the region $M \pm B/2$, declared by the market. The base
  fee there is zero throughout this piece.
- $Z$, the Maker Zone: the working radius past the band edge.
- $k_1$, the Zone Slope: fee per bps of distance inside the zone.
- $k_2$, the Far Slope: takes over past the zone edge and runs the fee to the
  cap.
- $F$, the Fee Cap: the taker rate. No resting order ever pays more than a
  taker.

And the one value belonging to the order being priced:

- $d$: an order's distance, in bps, beyond the band edge. It starts counting
  at $B/2$ from $M$, and inside the band it is zero. The fee is a function of
  $d$ and nothing else.

Put together, the base fee for a single order is:

$$
\text{base fee}(d) = \min\bigl(F,\; k_1 \cdot \min(d, Z) + k_2 \cdot \max(0,\, d - Z)\bigr)
$$

Read left to right: distance inside the zone is charged at $k_1$, distance
beyond it at $k_2$, and the total is clipped at $F$. Inside the band, $d$ is
zero, so the fee is zero.

Alongside the fee, the lab below plots a second curve: the net edge, an
order's distance from the mark minus the fee charged at that distance.

An order's distance can be viewed as roughly the edge it is asking for: if it fills, it earns that many bps of price improvement
relative to $M$. The net edge is what that improvement is worth once the
schedule takes its share. This is an
approximation, not a profit model; it ignores fill probability, adverse
selection, and inventory, all of which matter more. But it is the right lens
for seeing what the schedule rewards. With $k_1$ below 1, a wider quote still
nets more edge per fill, just less per bps. And once the fee reaches $F$,
the schedule stops caring about distance entirely: past that point, every
placement is priced like a market order.

## The Order Fee

The base fee set the floor: the lowest rate an order can pay at its distance.
What an order actually pays comes down to one rule:

> Every resting dollar of notional is either paired, with a dollar on the
> other side of the book behind it, or unpaired. A paired dollar pays the worse of its two
> legs' base fees. An unpaired dollar pays the cap, the same rate a market
> order pays.

A market functions when there is something to trade against at any
moment, in either direction.
While directional traders may, in aggregate, meet some of these liquidity needs, that happens only by coincidence of order timing, not from any commitment to provide liquidity. In contrast, non-directional traders (market makers) provide quotes standing on both sides of the book at the same time. This non-directional liquidity is what lets a market function continuously, and it is this type of liquidity that the fee schedule discounts.

Non-directional liquidity is determined from the sealed order book at auction
close: a trader's notional dollars on the bid side pair against their dollars
on the ask, best-priced first, until the smaller side runs out. Charging each
paired dollar its worse leg is what keeps the rule honest: a tight bid cannot
borrow a better rate than the far ask standing behind it. A resting order
with nothing behind it on the other side is fundamentally directional: it
demands the same one-way trade a market order demands, just at a limit price,
and it is priced at the same rate.

The calculated fee rate is charged only when the underlying liquidity
actually trades. The formula for the order fee is:

$$
\text{order fee} = \frac{\sum_{\text{slices}} q_i \cdot \max(s,\, s_i) \;+\; u \cdot F}{q}
$$

where:

- $q_i$, a slice: the portion of the order paired against a single
  opposite-side level.
- $s$: the order's own base-fee stamp.
- $s_i$: the stamp of the level the slice paired against.
- $u$: the notional left unpaired.
- $q$: the order's total size.

The formula is the combination of the base fee and the pairing rule. For a maker to lower its fee rate, it should place orders closer to the mark, where the
stamp $s$ is smaller, and size them to what the other side of the book can
pair, so nothing lands in $u$ paying the cap.

Since all stamps are set by their relative distance from the mark, $M$, how the mark is actually calculated needs to be covered next.

## Calculating the Mark

Every fee in the schedule is measured as a distance from a single reference
point: the mark, $M$, recomputed each window from the sealed book.

The midpoint of the best bid and best ask would be an obvious candidate to
set the mark, but it fails due to its blindness to size. The same midpoint
can be set just as easily by an order book with one dollar of bids at the
touch as by one with a million dollars there, even though the true cost of
trading against them is vastly different. This same blindness to size
makes the number cheap to move: a few dollars parked ahead of the real depth
set a new best price, and the midpoint follows. Every fee in the market would
hang from a number that costs almost nothing to move.

With the touch rejected, the next simplest reference would be to weigh every
resting order in the book. But most of that book is a poor signal. A
directional order is an opinion that the price should move: a lone bid below
the market is waiting for the price to come down to it, not a statement of
where trading stands. And the mark has no hold on it, since unpaired
liquidity pays the cap wherever the mark sits. Non-directional liquidity is
different on both counts. A trader quoting both sides is stating where they
believe the price stands, and their fees depend on the mark through both
legs, so their interest points at the mark landing near the center of their
own quotes. Less biased, not unbiased: their prices still set the mark. It is
also the consistent choice: the schedule discounts non-directional liquidity,
so the same liquidity supplies the reference the discount is measured from.

Within that liquidity, weight comes from size and placement: more notional is
a larger claim about where the asset trades, and notional standing closer to
the mid is a more confident one. Because everything that counts is a fillable
resting order, influencing the mark requires standing real, paired size that
anyone may trade against. The whole calculation can be thought of as an
election held each auction: non-directional traders (market makers) are the voters, and size
and proximity set the weight of each vote.

The calculation also has boundaries. Quotes count only within $B/2 + Z$ of
each side's best quote, half the Inner Band plus the Maker Zone: the same
working width the fee schedule discounts is the width the mark reads, which
is where the zone gets its name. The pairing must also happen inside that
area, on both sides. A tight bid whose only ask stands beyond the zone gets
no voice, even though the two still pair for fees; the fee's pairing accepts
any distance and prices it, while the mark's requires both legs to stand
within the area it measures.

The same width bounds the market itself. The best bid and best ask being
measured must stand within $B + 2Z$ of each other, the full working span.
Two quotes farther apart than that are not a market with a wide spread;
they are two claims about the price with no working region between them,
and the mark does not average them. A book that wide produces no
measurement at all.

The mark, then, is not calculated from the raw book. Per trader, keep only
the two-sided size that pairs within the area, and pool what survives by
price level. That filtered ladder is the eligible book, and it is the only
book the mark ever reads. Comparing prices across it requires a common size
to evaluate them at. $D$, the market's typical demand, is that basis (how
$D$ itself can be derived from the market's own activity, rather than set by
hand, comes later). Walk $D$ dollars of notional into the eligible bids, best
prices first, and take the volume-weighted average of everything the walk
consumed. That is the bid-side impact price, the price a seller of $D$
dollars would actually receive against the eligible liquidity. The ask side
gives its mirror, and the mark is their midpoint:

$$
M = \frac{\text{impactBid}(D) + \text{impactAsk}(D)}{2}
$$

Both impact prices are read from the eligible book alone; the rest of the
book never enters the formula. Measuring at size is what makes the mark
expensive to move. A tiny order
absorbs almost none of a $D$-dollar walk, so it barely moves the average;
shifting $M$ requires real size near the mid. If paired depth within the
measured area runs out before $D$ dollars, the missing dollars are priced at
the area's boundary; the measurement never reaches for size beyond it.

## Many Makers, One Mark

Everything so far ran on a single maker's book. In a real market the eligible
book pools every trader's paired, in-area size at once: the same filter and
the same walk, producing one mark for the whole market.

Within the pooled walk, a trader's voice is their share of the dollars it
consumed. Better-priced quotes are consumed first, and quotes at the same
price share pro-rata. A dollar is a dollar whoever stands it: a small maker's
size counts exactly in proportion, with no seniority and no minimum.

This produces a property worth stating plainly, because no existing fee
schedule has it: a maker does not set their fee. They set their book. The
formula is identical for everyone, but the mark inside it is calculated from
every maker's quotes at once, so the rate a given book pays depends on where
the rest of the market stands. The dependence shrinks with weight, and weight
comes from the same actions the schedule already rewards: quoting larger and
tighter pulls the mark toward the center of your own quotes, which is where
your rate is most predictable. The same behavior that earns the discount also
buys certainty about it.

Pooling has one more consequence. Makers usually cluster, everyone's quotes
within reach of everyone else's, and the filter produces a single eligible
book. But books can also stand so far apart that neither lies within the
other's measuring reach. Each region then forms its own candidate eligible
book, and the mark reads the largest one. The smaller candidate gets no
voice: its quotes are priced against a mark it had no hand in. Moving the
mark away from the market therefore has a single price: standing more
paired, fillable size than everyone else combined, within one working span.

Two situations produce no new mark. If no valid candidate stands anywhere,
or two candidates that share nothing stand at exactly equal size, the market
holds the last mark, flagged as held, until a dominant book returns. And a
market that has never had a mark holds nothing: from launch until the first
candidate forms there is no mark, and every fill pays the cap. The first
account to stand a real two-sided book within the span creates the mark, and
with it the first access to a discount.

The lab below is that market: your book on top, the aggregate of every other

away from the market and watch the larger book keep the mark. Then press
play and watch $M$ drift as the makers reprice, while your book stands still
and its receipts re-price around it.

## Measuring Typical Demand

To calculate the mark, the eligible book must be walked on both sides up to size $D$, the typical demand for that market. What remains is how that typical demand is determined.

Within any market, demand continuously changes. Therefore, $D$ should not be based upon some generic constant. Instead, it needs to reflect the market's conditions as they actually are.

Doing that well means satisfying two criteria:

1. $D$ should track the market's demand as it changes: responsive enough to
   follow a real shift within days, steady enough that the measurement
   itself does not become a source of noise.
2. $D$ should be trustless: computed from published data by a fixed rule,
   predictable and verifiable, as every other number in the design already
   is.

What follows is one mechanism that satisfies both. Unlike the fee rule and
the mark, it is not settled design: it is a demonstration of one possible solution. If preferred, other mechanisms could serve in its place.

### A Proposed Mechanism for Measuring Demand

The mechanism runs on two clocks:

1. **Every window**: the market's fills add to
   two running sums.
2. **Every day**: the sums are used to compute the typical demand for that day and $D$ then takes one bounded step toward it.

When the day is done, the sums reset to zero and start accumulating for the new day.

In full, the mechanism takes eight values, of three kinds.

The value measured every window:

- $v$: a window's volume, corresponding to the actual notional dollar volume that executed against the
  standing book in that window, both directions summed. Because $v$ is read
  from fills, it adapts to the venue's design: in a dual-flow auction every
  taker dollar trades against the book, while in a single-cross auction
  buys and sells net against each other first and only the imbalance
  reaches the book, which is then genuinely what the book absorbed. $D$ is
  the typical window's demand, not the typical order's size.

The two sums carried through the day:

- $S_1$: the sum of every window's $v$, the day's total flow.
- $S_2$: the sum of every window's $v^2$. Together the two sums give the
  day's reading of typical demand, $S_2/S_1$.

And the market's configuration, fixed from day to day:

- $\alpha$: the chase speed, how much of the gap between $D$ and the day's
  reading a single day may close.
- $m$: the minimum sample. A day carrying less than $m \cdot D$ of flow
  does not update.
- $L$: the daily limit. One day moves $D$ by at most a factor of $L$,
  in either direction.
- $D_{\min}$: the floor, the smallest value $D$ may take.
- $D_0$: the seed, $D$'s value at launch. It initializes and constrains
  nothing afterward.

Put together, the daily update is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{step} &= \min\!\left(L,\; \max\!\left(\tfrac{1}{L},\; \left(\tfrac{S_2/S_1}{D}\right)^{\alpha}\right)\right) \\[8pt]
D &\leftarrow
\begin{cases}
\max\left(D_{\min},\; D \cdot \text{step}\right) & \text{if } S_1 \geq m \cdot D \\
D & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

The step line reads from the inside out: $S_2/S_1$ is where the day says
demand stands, its ratio against $D$ is the gap, $\alpha$ sets how much of
that gap one day may close, and the $\min$ and $\max$ hold the result to
$[1/L,\ L]$. The second line applies it: a day that carried the minimum
sample moves $D$ by the step, never below the floor, and a day that did
not leaves $D$ unchanged.

#### The Reasoning

**The dollar-weighted average.** Consider a fictitious day with two windows
of trading, one clearing \$2,000 and one clearing \$8,000. There are two
ways to average them. Averaged over windows, the typical window carried
\$5,000. Averaged over dollars, the answer is different: of the \$10,000
that traded, four of every five dollars traded in the larger window, so the
typical traded dollar sat in a window of
$0.2 \times 2000 + 0.8 \times 8000 = 6800$ dollars. $S_2/S_1$ computes the
second average
($S_1 = 2000 + 8000 = 10000$ and $S_2 = 2000^2 + 8000^2 = 68 \times 10^6$),
and it is the one the walk needs, because the walk's question is what it
costs to trade where the trading actually is. It is also inexpensive to
maintain: computing it requires no count of windows, an empty window adds
nothing to either sum, and a window that traded costs only two additions to
record.

**The fractional chase.** The exponent controls how quickly $D$ chases the
day's reading. At $\alpha = 1$ the update would jump the whole way in a
single day, and $D$ would inherit each day's noise along with its signal.
Below one, the update closes only part of the gap and leaves the rest for
the days that follow. At $\alpha = 1/2$, a reading at four times $D$ steps
it up by $\sqrt{4} = 2$, and the gap that remains falls to its square root
each day after; at $\alpha = 3/4$ the same reading steps it by
$4^{3/4} \approx 2.8$ and converges in fewer days. Where in that range a
market should sit is a calibration choice, and the design does not fix it.

**The minimum sample and the daily limit.** These two conditions protect
the tracking from days that would mislead it. The minimum sample keeps a
dead day from re-measuring anything: a day with only a handful of fills
says very little about typical demand, so $D$ holds unchanged rather than
update from it, and nothing decays while it waits. The daily limit caps how
far any single day can move $D$, however strange that day's trading was, a
single enormous window included.

**The floor.** The floor exists for the mark's sake rather than for the
tracking. $D$ sets the depth the mark's walk measures at, and a walk that
has shrunk too far reads only the quotes at the touch, which is the
size-blind midpoint the mark was designed to avoid in the first place.

## Which Auction Design Fits Best

<div class="stub">

- Placement TBD (likely here). The intro gives DFBA only a cameo; this section carries the full description and the argument
- The netting weakness of a single shared cross, told here: in a CLOB a buy and a sell each pay the spread; in one batch cross they net at the clearing price and the standing book is paid only on the imbalance. Thins the reason to be the standing book, which is what the market leans on when flow turns one-sided; effect grows with window length
- DFBA mechanics in full (moved from the setting section): declared flows (intent, not urgency; orders on either flow can rest across windows), two simultaneous auctions per window (100ms to 1s), uniform price crossing the most volume, pro-rata at ties, makers never trade with makers; 250ms human reaction time makes the cadence feel instantaneous
- DFBA keeps the sealed window and removes the netting: every taker fill pays a standing quote, in either direction, at any window length
- Because taker buys and sells never net, gross urgency walks the maker book. That is the venue type the gentle stamp slope is calibrated for; in netting venues deep excursions collapse and the calibration must be refit
- The maker flow is an explicit standing book: exactly the object the snapshot reads
- Pro-rata at equal prices in DFBA matching rhymes with the attribution-free, pro-rata mark walk: a shared fairness grammar
- Declared roles vs measured behavior: DFBA makes maker/taker an explicit declaration; snapshot fees reads directionality from the book. It can price within the declared maker flow, or dissolve the declaration entirely
- Plain FBA: the formula ports verbatim (roles never appear in it); the calibration does not, and should be fit to the venue's sweep-depth distribution

## Concerns, Variations, Open Questions

<div class="stub">

- Fee uncertainty as a cost of the design: on every existing venue a maker knows their rate before quoting; here the rate is known only up to the mark, so it becomes another variable to model alongside fill probability and adverse selection, with a confidence interval rather than a number. Mitigations: the schedule and formula are fully public; the worst case is bounded at the cap $F$; and unlike order flow, the interval is under the maker's own control, since weight (size and tightness) narrows it. Expect pushback here; treat it as a real trade-off, not a non-issue
- Semi-far pairing earns a discount: paid optionality, priced by fill risk and adverse selection
- Manipulation economics: junk pairing is worthless, bending $M$ requires standing real paired size. Under candidate selection this is winner-take-all: moving or freezing the mark requires out-sizing the honest book's eligible size within one $B + 2Z$ span, and any size short of that buys exactly zero influence. The old dust-freeze hole (tiny far paired size grabbing an anchor and freezing $M$) is closed by the same rule
- Parameters and governance: $B$ declared and sticky, $F$ venue-wide, $Z$ and the two slopes shape the curve, $D$ measured from demand
- Volume pricing composes rather than competes: volume (like staking in the spec) can lower an account's cap $F$ toward a floor. A 10bps cap might fall to 2.5bps on volume alone, but the last mile to zero is quality only. The floored cap preserves day-one competition for new makers and gives high-volume takers a legible benefit
- Variations considered: partner-stamp grace interval, asymptotic stamps (rejected: softens the junk anchor), inside compensation (parked), partial fills, blending $M$ between near-tied rival candidate books (rejected: partial size must buy zero influence, and a blended mark stands at a price where no liquidity does)
- Band fee level as a parameter: add an intercept $c$ to the base fee, $\min(F,\ c + k_1 \cdot \min(d, Z) + k_2 \cdot \max(0,\ d - Z))$; the band pays exactly $c$, and the piece's design is the special case $c = 0$. Negative $c$ is a rebate for the tightest quotes. It leaks smoothly past the band edge (fee crosses zero at $d = -c/k_1$), needs $c > -F$ so a self-cross costs $F + c > 0$ (the taker cap bounds the rebate against wash trading), and reopens the standard maker-rebate manipulation literature that $c = 0$ dismisses in one sentence
- Crossed books, by size of cross (needs its own beat, possibly with Which Auction Design): a cross smaller than the eligibility reach ($Z + B/2$) computes normally; impact prices cross, the spread exports negative, $M$ sits mid-cross, and aggressive quotes stamp at zero (aggression is never punished). A cross or gap larger than the reach splits the snapshot into rival candidate eligible books and the mark reads the largest; the smaller book keeps pairing for fees but has no voice. Overlapping candidates (the chain case: A within reach of B, B within reach of C, A and C not) are two readings of one region and resolve to the larger with a deterministic tiebreak, never held. In a DFBA makers never trade each other, so crossed maker books persist and drain via taker flow; note the no-arbitrage band: crosses narrower than roughly 2F are not arbitrageable (two taker caps exceed the edge), just better prices for natural flow
- The no-mark states (needs its own beat): three states, none / fresh / held, and no declared launch mark. From launch until the first valid candidate book forms there is no mark and every fill pays the cap $F$. A free launch window was considered and rejected: two sybil accounts, one bidding and one asking, would wash-trade fee-free until a mark formed; charging the cap instead makes standing the first real two-sided book the way to unlock discounts. held only bridges windows after a mark has existed (no valid candidate, or a strict tie between disjoint candidates) and is unreachable at launch by construction. fresh vs held vs healthy (impact spread against $B$) are three different gates and consumers choose which to require. Open: whether held should expire at some age or carry indefinitely with consumers gating on staleness
- Liquidations as a future mark consumer (out of scope here, but it constrains the design now): a liquidation reference would be an EMA or TWAP of recent fresh-and-healthy marks with a staleness bound, never the raw per-window $M$, so sustaining a manipulation across the lookback means being the market for its whole length. Mark availability becomes a design metric: the fraction of windows fresh and healthy, especially under stress, since the span check can empty the mark exactly when volatility widens every book; profiles must size $B + 2Z$ against stressed spreads, not calm ones. Natural listing rule: leverage enables only after the mark has some window count of fresh, healthy history
- Open: cross-window dynamics, staking-based fee caps, thin-market $M$ fallbacks
- Some ideas: make the cap itself volume derived but let it lower down to a floor. Still retains snapshot fees as the dominant driver as fees for makers. Example, 8bps Fee Cap. Cap can lower based on volume to 2bps. Still allows new makers to compete on pricing and inventory management.
- Need to show the O complexity of what it costs to derive the Mark and the fee schedule per window. It's worthwhile to show how much it costs computationally. Candidate discovery adds one eligibility pass per two-sided account (seeds dedupe, and in a coherent market they all converge to one candidate).
- Need a section to walk through/show dynamically deriving D over time.
- The dynamically calibrated market (likely its own late section, "Calibrating the Market" / "Market Profiles", holding the dynamic-$D$ walkthrough too). The cadence ladder: every parameter changes at the slowest cadence that keeps it honest — $M$ per window (it is the measurement), $D$ daily (a statistic needs a sample; one window must never move the yardstick that prices it), $B$/$Z$/$k_1$/$k_2$ on a ~30-day lookback in discrete steps, $F$ slowest as venue policy
- Market profiles: instead of infinite parameter permutations, 3–5 vetted coherent bundles (the parameters interact: full fee at $B/2 + Z + (F - k_1 Z)/k_2$), expected to correlate with volume and volatility. Anchor for the selector: $B$ should cover noise, not signal — e.g. a quantile of window-to-window $M$ drift; high volatility → wider band, gentler slopes (width there is insurance, not laziness); deep high-volume markets → tight band, stricter precision pricing. Shared profiles also pool statistics across the long tail instead of overfitting thin markets
- Profiles shift like gears, not fluid drift: legibility (makers precompute economics per profile once), hysteresis (two thresholds so a boundary market never gear-hunts), and manipulation resistance (moving a parameter requires sustaining a 30-day distortion — the mark's "only way to fake it is to do the job" defense at a longer timescale). Selection can run off-chain, but its inputs and rule are published so anyone can verify the current gear; shifts announced with a notice period
- Transition cost owed to the reader: a gear shift reprices resting books mid-flight (cheap stamps under old $B$ may stamp expensive under the new); scheduled transition windows, possibly one window of grandfathering
