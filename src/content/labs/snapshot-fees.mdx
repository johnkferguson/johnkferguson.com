## Understanding Batch Auctions

Most electronic markets, crypto exchanges included, run on a continuous limit
order book (CLOB). Orders rest in a book sorted by price, and when a new order
crosses the spread it trades immediately against the best resting quote. Ties
at the same price go to whoever arrived first. In a continuous limit order book, every fill, quote, and cancel is processed one at a time, in arrival order.

Processing by arrival order creates a clear incentive: if the market moves, be the first
to react. When a news event or external factor changes
the fair price, a market maker's resting quote can become stale. If it does, a race begins. Whoever hits that stale
quote first can extract profit from its mispricing. Inside the race, the maker's cancel is a single message trying to outpace every sniper's order. More often than not, the maker's stale quote gets picked off. These latency races, often referred to as sniping or latency arbitrage, are a form of
adverse selection that concentrates on market makers. In response, makers size down and widen their tightest quotes, and
this thinner, widened spread is an added cost paid by everyone trading for ordinary reasons.

[Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/137/1/493/6368348)
measured the race directly, using exchange message data that records the
losers as well as the winners. In FTSE 100 stocks they found races running
about once per minute per symbol, typically decided in five to ten microseconds, accounting for roughly 20% of trading volume, with the top six
firms on one side or the other of more than 80% of them. Eliminating latency
arbitrage, they estimate, would cut the market's cost of liquidity by 17%,
about $5 billion a year in global equities. This additional liquidity cost is structurally inherent to
the design of continuous order books.

Frequent batch auctions are the design change
[Budish, Cramton, and Shim proposed](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/130/4/1547/1916146):
instead of processing orders continuously, collect them for a short window,
seal it, and clear every crossing order at one uniform price. Inside a window
there is no first. A momentarily stale quote is no longer a prize for the
fastest, because while the auction window is open, everyone has the same
chance to react. A microsecond-level latency advantage no longer matters. With that form of adverse selection neutralized, a maker can quote
tighter and with larger size.

The design space has kept moving since. Jump Crypto's 2025
[dual-flow batch auction](https://jumpcrypto.com/resources/dual-flow-batch-auction)
splits each window into two auctions, maker bids against taker sells and maker
asks against taker buys, so taker flow always trades against standing quotes
instead of netting against itself. While the _Snapshot Fees_ mechanism proposed in this piece could work for any batch auction implementation, it is likely a more natural fit for a dual-flow batch auction. The reasoning behind that will be covered later.

Batch auctions improve upon continuous books by neutralizing latency arbitrage and the widened spreads it forces. Structurally, operating within discrete time windows provides an additional feature continuous books cannot offer: a clean snapshot of the market's state, with each order in it committed and fillable at that instant. This piece asks whether that snapshot could be used to price orders directly, and whether pricing them well could improve the market further, with even tighter quotes and more size behind them.

## Trading Fees Today

Exchanges everywhere charge for execution, and nearly everywhere the same two
ideas shape the price: liquidity provision is treated better than liquidity
demand, and activity is rewarded, with rates that fall as volume grows. In
traditional markets these arrangements are more layered and bespoke, running
through negotiated maker programs and rebate schedules that vary by venue and
firm. This piece focuses on crypto markets, where fee structures are simpler
and public, already quoted in bps, and the most congruent with what it
proposes.

There, the typical schedule is compact: a maker/taker split, where takers pay
a few bps of notional and makers pay less, sometimes nothing, occasionally a
rebate. Tiers then scale those rates with each account's trailing volume. As a design this
is a reasonable proxy: volume is easy to measure and roughly tracks how much
value a participant brings to a venue. On a continuous book it is also close
to the only thing an exchange can price. There is no moment when the market
stands still to be judged, so fees attach to the one thing that accumulates:
what you traded.

Batch auctions do have such a moment: every window ends with the market
sealed and judgeable. Conventional fee standards like volume-based pricing
still work here, but the sealed window makes a different kind of pricing
possible. Jump Crypto's dual-flow paper alludes to this: maker fees could be
priced by how far an order stands from the midpoint. This piece takes that
idea and expands it into a full pricing mechanism.

## The Idea: Directionality Is the Price

- Replace maker/taker with a spectrum of directionality
- Confidently two-sided near fair value: trades free. Fully one-sided: pays the taker rate
- A one-sided resting order is a patient taker
- Everything is computed fresh from one sealed snapshot per window: memoryless, verifiable, no tiers, no identity

## The Base Fee: Pricing Distance from the Mark

The base fee prices a single order by one measure: how far it stands from the
market's Mark Price: `M`.

- `M`: the Mark price; a more accurate midpoint for the market. Liquidity standing in the inner band and Maker Zone contributes to its calculation. how comes later.
- `B`: the Inner Band: the region `M ± B/2`, declared by the market. The base fee there is zero, so `d` starts counting at the band edge, `B/2` from `M`.
- `Z`, the Maker Zone: the working radius past the band edge. Inside it, distance is priced gently.
- `k₁`, the zone slope: fee per bps of distance inside the zone.
- `k₂`, the far slope: takes over past the zone edge and runs the fee to
  the cap.
- `F`, the fee cap: the taker rate. No resting order ever pays more than a taker.

```
d = bps beyond the band edge (0 if inside the band)

base fee(d) = min(F, k₁ × min(d, Z) + k₂ × max(0, d − Z))
```

<div class="stub">

- Net edge: distance earns 1:1 while the schedule takes its slope per bps; `k₁` below 1 keeps width profitable
- Full-fee point: once the fee reaches `F`, the schedule stops caring

</div>

## The Mark Is Communal

<div class="stub">

## Which Auction Design Fits Best

<div class="stub">

- Placement TBD (likely here). The intro gives DFBA only a cameo; this section carries the full description and the argument
- The netting weakness of a single shared cross, told here: in a CLOB a buy and a sell each pay the spread; in one batch cross they net at the clearing price and the standing book is paid only on the imbalance. Thins the reason to be the standing book, which is what the market leans on when flow turns one-sided; effect grows with window length
- DFBA mechanics in full (moved from the setting section): declared flows (intent, not urgency; orders on either flow can rest across windows), two simultaneous auctions per window (100ms to 1s), uniform price crossing the most volume, pro-rata at ties, makers never trade with makers; 250ms human reaction time makes the cadence feel instantaneous
- DFBA keeps the sealed window and removes the netting: every taker fill pays a standing quote, in either direction, at any window length
- Because taker buys and sells never net, gross urgency walks the maker book. That is the venue type the gentle stamp slope is calibrated for; in netting venues deep excursions collapse and the calibration must be refit
- The maker flow is an explicit standing book: exactly the object the snapshot reads
- Pro-rata at equal prices in DFBA matching rhymes with the attribution-free, pro-rata mark walk: a shared fairness grammar
- Declared roles vs measured behavior: DFBA makes maker/taker an explicit declaration; snapshot fees reads directionality from the book. It can price within the declared maker flow, or dissolve the declaration entirely
- Plain FBA: the formula ports verbatim (roles never appear in it); the calibration does not, and should be fit to the venue's sweep-depth distribution

</div>

## Concerns, Variations, Open Questions

<div class="stub">

- Semi-far pairing earns a discount: paid optionality, priced by fill risk and adverse selection
- Manipulation economics: junk pairing is worthless, bending `M` requires standing real paired size
- Parameters and governance: `B` declared and sticky, `F` venue-wide, `Z` and the two slopes shape the curve, `T` measured from demand
- Volume pricing composes rather than competes: volume (like staking in the spec) can lower an account's cap `F` toward a floor. A 10bps cap might fall to 2.5bps on volume alone, but the last mile to zero is quality only. The floored cap preserves day-one competition for new makers and gives high-volume takers a legible benefit
- Variations considered: partner-stamp grace interval, asymptotic stamps (rejected: softens the junk anchor), inside compensation (parked), partial fills
- Open: cross-window dynamics, staking-based fee caps, thin-market `M` fallbacks
- Some ideas: make the cap itself volume derived but let it lower down to a floor. Still retains snapshot fees as the dominant driver as fees for makers. Example, 8bps Fee Cap. Cap can lower based on volume to 2bps. Still allows new makers to compete on pricing and inventory management.

</div>
